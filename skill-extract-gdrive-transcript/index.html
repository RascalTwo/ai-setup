↓  WHY IT EXISTS

The transcript is already there

A Google Meet recording on Drive plays in Google's own viewer, and that viewer has a Transcript panel: auto-generated captions, timestamped, with speaker names attached. The whole premise of this skill is that you don't need to get the video out of Google to get the words out of the video. It drives Chrome, reads the panel's DOM, and writes a WebVTT file.

What that buys you: no OAuth flow, no Drive API, no download, no FFmpeg, no transcription run. What it costs you: three hard requirements. Chrome MCP has to be connected, you have to be logged into Google in that Chrome with access to the recording, and the recording has to actually have captions.

Hand it a Drive URL

One argument, and it is the link you already have open. The skill fires on a Drive recording URL, or on any way of asking for the words out of a meeting — "get me a transcript of this", "download the meeting notes", "who said what" — even when nobody says the word transcript.

https://drive.google.com/file/d/{fileId}/view

No URL given, it asks for one. And it asks you to be signed in before it starts: a login or access-denied screen is a full stop, not something to work around.

The things that go wrong

  • Very few entries for a long video — the panel lazy-loads. Scroll it, then re-extract.
  • Panel opens empty — captions may still be processing on Google's side. Try later.
  • Zero timestamp elements found — the DOM has moved. Fall back to reading the panel visually and adapt.
  • Wrong or missing speaker names — Meet's speaker detection isn't perfect. The skill doesn't pretend to fix that; you edit the file.

Three standing refusals, on top of those. It never signs you in — no credential handling, no OAuth, no token; it uses the session your Chrome already has or it stops. It never downloads the video, which is the point. And it never invents a transcript: when Google has no captions, it says so and hands off rather than quietly re-recognising the audio and presenting the result as Google's.

And the boundary: this one is for a recording sitting behind the Drive player. If the video is already on disk, that's extract-video-subtitles' job, not this one.

Where it lives

Requireschrome and claude-in-chrome (the Chrome MCP connection). Everything happens through javascript_tool, find/left_click, and screenshots in the tab you are already logged into.
Also needsA Google session in that Chrome with access to the recording, and a recording that has captions — Meet enables them by default, but a recording made with them off has nothing to read.
Hands off totranscribe-media, and only when Google's captions are proven absent.
Its stateNone. The manifest declares state: false and the skill has nowhere to keep anything: it is a sequence of instructions executed against a live page. No cache of discovered class names, no record of which recordings it has read, nothing under ~/.agents/. It keeps nothing between runs — which is why step 4 below re-discovers the DOM every single time rather than remembering last week's answer.
What it writesOne .vtt, into your Downloads folder, by the browser's own download mechanism. Named from document.title, slugged. That is output, not state — and it is written by Chrome, not by the skill.
Safe to deleteThe .vtt, whenever you like; re-running re-extracts it. There is nothing else to delete.
Your sessionUntouched. It reads the page you are logged into and never stores, copies or forwards a cookie or credential.

Step 1 — the panel does not exist until the video does

The panel frequently isn't there — or is there and empty — until the video has started. So the flow is: navigate, screenshot, hit play, wait three to five seconds, pause, screenshot again. A login or access-denied screen is a full stop; the skill tells you to sign in rather than trying to work around it.

Step 2 — the greyed-out button, and why the click lies

A recording with no captions still gets a Transcript bar, and the button in it is disabled — which does not stop a find from matching it or a left_click from landing on it. An agent that trusts the click will sit there retrying and DOM-hunting for a panel that cannot open. The check is explicit and it happens before the click.
// run this first — the click will lie to you, the property won't
const t = Array.from(document.querySelectorAll('button'))
  .find(b => b.textContent.trim() === 'Transcript');
JSON.stringify({ found: !!t, disabled: t?.disabled,
                 ariaDisabled: t?.getAttribute('aria-disabled') });

Both properties are read because either can carry the state: a native disabled on a real <button>, or aria-disabled="true" on a styled one. If either says so, the browser flow is over: no clicks, no retries, no DOM discovery. There is no Google transcript to extract, and every further step is wasted motion.

Step 3 — what it does instead of giving up

Absent captions still leave you a recording full of speech. So the skill hands off: download the file from Drive and run transcribe-media over it.

skills/transcribe-media/transcribe.sh <downloaded-file> --format vtt

Same output shape, different provenance — the words are there, but the speaker names aren't, because those came from Google, not from the audio. This is a genuine fallback, not an alternative: it only fires when Google's own captions are proven absent.

Step 4 — nothing about the DOM is written down

Google's class names are obfuscated and change periodically, so hardcoding them would produce a skill that works until it silently doesn't. Instead it walks document.querySelectorAll('*'), keeps the leaf elements (el.children.length === 0) whose entire text matches /^\d{1,2}:\d{2}$/, and reads three class names off the survivors and their siblings:

ENTRY_CLASS      — the parent wrapping one transcript entry
TIMESTAMP_CLASS  — the child holding just "0:08"
TEXT_CLASS       — the sibling holding "(Speaker)\ntext"

With one deliberate discard: skip the first two matches. Those are the player's own current-time and duration readouts, which are also leaf elements containing exactly a timestamp. Real entries are the ones whose parent class repeats across many elements.

Step 5 — parsing, and the speaker label that isn't speech

Equally unglamorous and equally specific. The speaker name arrives in parentheses and repeats inside the same block — it's a label, not speech. So it is captured once with /^\(([^)]+)\)/, stripped everywhere with replace(/\([^)]+\)\n?/g, ''), and re-emitted as a WebVTT <v> tag. An entry with no name inherits lastSpeaker. Timestamps are widened to VTT's hh:mm:ss.mmm by padding, each cue ends where the next one begins, and the final cue is padded to 59:59 because the panel never says when the last line stopped.

Step 6 — why the file comes out of the browser, not the tool

The VTT is built in the page and downloaded via a Blob, an object URL, and a synthetic <a download> click, so it lands in your Downloads folder. That's not decoration — returning a long transcript as a JavaScript return value gets it truncated. The Blob route sidesteps the size limit entirely; only a small receipt comes back, and the object URL is revoked immediately after the click. The report to you is that receipt: entry count, the distinct speakers, the filename, and the time range covered.
