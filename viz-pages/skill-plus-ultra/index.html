The list that always fills

"Could this be better?" is a question a model essentially never answers no to. Asked in prose it produces a list of improvements every single time, and you can't tell which two entries are load-bearing and which five are there to look responsive. The whole answer degrades into noise.

So the work of this skill isn't generating improvements — that part is free and worthless. It's making each one earn its place. "Nothing — ship it" is a valid output, and sometimes the correct one.

Debts before ideas

It opens by remembering, not brainstorming. Whoever just did the work is already sitting on a ledger of compromises they made and moved past:

  • Scope trimmed to fit — "the simple version for now".
  • TODO, FIXME, stubs, hardcoded values, magic numbers.
  • Error paths left unhandled; edge cases acknowledged and skipped.
  • Tests unwritten or written shallow — a manual check standing in for an automated one.
  • A workaround chosen over the real fix, and the reason.
  • Anything the work rests on that was assumed rather than verified.

This is the highest-value material in the skill, and the part you cannot get by asking the question conversationally — a cold brainstorm reaches for what's interesting rather than what's actually missing. Only once the ledger is exhausted does it invent: the adjacent capability, the direction the work could take. Ideas go second on purpose, because they crowd out debts whenever they go first.

If the work isn't in context — an older change, a different session — it reads the artifact before saying anything: the diff, the files, the test output, the issue it came from. Speculating about code it hasn't looked at is exactly how the filler gets in.

A price, or an admission

Each entry carries an effort estimate and what it buys. The difference is not cosmetic:

✓ add retry with backoff · ~20 min · removes the most likely production page
✗ improve error handling

The second one hands the entire judgment back to you — which is the labour you invoked this to be spared. Where it genuinely can't estimate, it says so in place of the price rather than dropping the price silently.

Why the buckets don't merge

The sorting test is a single question: was it promised? Promised-and-missing is a debt; never-promised is upside. They stay apart because they're answered by different questions — a debt is a risk call, an upside is a value call. Merged into one ranked list, a shiny idea outranks a missing error handler and you have to re-sort by hand, which is the labour this exists to spare you.

The third bucket does something the other two can't do for themselves. Naming what was rejected, and why, is the clearest available evidence that the model was selecting rather than listing — and it's often the most useful section on the page, because it tells you which tempting directions are traps.

The trap: treating everything ponytail cut as a debt. Ponytail runs by default on coding work and strips scope — YAGNI, stdlib over custom, one line over fifty. This skill is its opposite lens, which is precisely why it's invoked rather than standing: the two cannot run at once. But a deferral that was correct stays correct, and re-listing it turns plus-ultra into an undo button for the skill that just did its job. Deliberately deferred scope belongs under Upside only if the reason to defer it has since weakened — and nowhere at all if it hasn't.

When the buckets come out thin

The lazy default is reviewing your own work in-context: free, instant, usually enough. Its weakness is that the author of a piece of work is the worst-positioned party to see what it's missing — you already judged each of those calls fine once.

So when the buckets look thin, or suspiciously flattering, it escalates: an independent subagent that hasn't seen the reasoning, handed only the artifact and the goal it was meant to serve. What that agent flags and you didn't is precisely the material your own investment hid. Reserved for work that matters — on a small change, the in-context pass is the right amount of effort.

Then it stops

It closes on a single call: the one thing to do next, or "nothing — ship it." A call, not a summary of the buckets. This skill proposes; it does not execute. It waits for you to choose before building anything.

Where it sits

Three lenses, three moments, and the timing is the whole distinction. steelman tests a decision before you commit to it. grilling shapes a plan before it's built. plus-ultra fires after the work is declared done — it doesn't shape the plan and it doesn't re-litigate the decision.
