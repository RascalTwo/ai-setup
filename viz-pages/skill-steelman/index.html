The problem

Ask an AI whether your plan is good and it will tell you your plan is good. That agreement is cheapest exactly when the stakes are highest — you've already decided, you're invested in the conclusion, and now the one thing in the room that could talk you out of it is nodding along. That's not a flaw in you; it's how minds work. It's just useless.

When to fire it

Say steelman this, /steelman, "red-team my plan", "am I fooling myself" — or any homophone the transcriber produces. There are no arguments and no setup; the claim under debate is whatever you were just talking about. Hard-to-reverse or high-stakes calls:

  • An architectural pattern you'll live inside for a year.
  • A dependency choice.
  • A delete or migrate — the things you can't take back.
  • Picking a direction over expensive alternatives.

Works in both directions: before a decision it's a pre-mortem (catch it before you pay for it); after something's built it's a red-team. Skip trivial and reversible calls — proportionality. For small ones, the in-context pass is the right amount of effort.

It argues. It does not decide.

  • It never returns a winner. The prime directive is that you are the judge. It may offer one clearly-labeled tentative lean — "if forced, I'd lean X, because crux Y" — and that lean is subordinate to your call, never a replacement for it.
  • It does not act on the outcome. After the verdict it stops. Whatever the argument implied you should do, it waits for you to say so.
  • It will not interview you. Building the case is its job, not yours. It only comes back for a crux that hinges on something it genuinely cannot resolve — your risk tolerance, private context, plans it can't see — and then one at a time, not a barrage.
  • It won't self-invoke. No trigger inside the skill fires it; something outside has to. That gap is the reason it's pinned in AGENTS.md as a standing rule.
  • It is not the planning pass. Shaping a plan is grilling's job. This one pressure-tests a decision that already exists.

How it's wired in

A steelman won't self-invoke — which is exactly why it's a standing rule rather than a skill you're trusted to remember. AGENTS.md §3 (Planning & Verification) makes it mandatory before any hard-to-reverse or high-stakes decision.

The turn

Steelman flips the role. The AI is the advocate; you are the judge. It builds the case for the way its most capable proponent would, then the case against at equal strength — the best objection, the failure mode, the thing you're motivated not to see. No strawmen. Then it names the cruxes: the load-bearing assumptions that, if flipped, flip the answer.

Six steps, in order, from a sentence you haven't sharpened yet to a verdict that is yours to give. Green is what the step produces; red is what it is forbidden to do there.

Everything resolvable is resolved before you're touched

Step 2 is the one that keeps the skill from becoming an interview. Every fact the claim depends on gets looked up first — the codebase, the web, memory — and only the assumptions survive into step 5 as candidate cruxes. By the time a question reaches you, it is a question nothing else in the room could have answered.

When the opposition feels soft: one mind arguing with itself can produce a polite, symmetric both-sides where nothing lands. On calls that matter, step 4 escalates — spawning an independent subagent whose only mandate is to kill the idea. A prosecutor with no obligation to be fair. Its best shots are fed back into the against-case; its independence is the whole value.
